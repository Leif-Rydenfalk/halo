# WakeLoc: Ultra-Low Power On-Demand UWB RTLS using Wake-Up Radios (arXiv:2504.20545) - excerpts

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.20545 / https://arxiv.org/html/2504.20545v1
INFOCOM WKSHPS 2025 (NetRobiCS). Fetched: 2026-09-03

```
 Abstract: For future large scale robotic moon missions, the availability of infrastructure-less, cheap and low power real-time locating systems (RTLSs) is critical. Traditional RTLS face significant trade-offs between power consumption and localization latency, often requiring anchors to be connected to the power grid or sacrificing speed for energy efficiency. This paper proposes WakeLoc, an on-demand RTLS based on ultra-wideband (UWB), enabling both low-latency and ultra-low power consumption by leveraging UWB wake-up radios (WuRs). In WakeLoc, tags independently start a localization procedure by sending a wake-up call (WuC) to anchors, before performing the actual localization. Distributed tags equipped with WuRs listen to the WuC and use passive listening of the UWB messages to determine their own position. Experimental measurements demonstrate that the localization accuracy in a 2D setup achieves less than 12.9cm error, both for the active and the passive tag. Additional power simulations based on real-world measurements were performed in a realistic environment, showing that anchors can achieve a power consumption as low as 15.53{\mu}W while the RTLS performs one on-demand localization per minute for 5 tags, thus operate up to 5.01 years on a single coin cell battery (690mWh).
```

## Hardware, accuracy and measured energy
```
 IV-A Node Hardware and Firmware 
 The anchor and tag nodes consist of the same hardware, a Qorvo DWS3000 UWB transceiver together with an STMicroelectronics Nucleo-L476RG microcontroller. As the promising WuR of Villani et al.  [ 16 ] is not commercially available yet, the system was emulated using WuC generated over wires via GPIO ( GPIO ) interrupts. 
 The firmware for the nodes ensures that the MCU is always in ’Stop 2’ sleep-state when waiting for interrupts, or actively working otherwise. 
 The parameters of WakeLoc are tuned to minimize energy consumption, while still fulfilling timing requirements for the localization. The time between the transmission of the initial poll message and the anchors response is set to Δ ​ T A i = 720   µ ​ s + 230   µ ​ s ⋅ i \Delta T_{A_{i}}=$720\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}$+$230\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}$\cdot i . A wake-up time of 500   µ ​ s 500\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s} is assumed, once the WuC has been sent. Using a WuC duration of 55   ms 55\text{\,}\mathrm{ms} , the latency results in ≈ 60   ms \approx$60\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}$ for N = 5 N=5 anchors.

 IV-C Simulation 
 To determine the power consumption, the real-world scenario was simulated using Python. 
 IV-C 1 Node model 
 To model the nodes, the power measurements described in  Section   IV-B were accumulated along with the durations of the individual component’s active states. Afterwards, the energy consumption per localization event was evaluated for each node type. The energy consumption itself is modeled depending on the number of anchors and the transmission distance of each node. 
 IV-C 2 Environment models 
 The simulation is based on the node distribution of the Cloves testbed  [ 19 ] . The simulated environment consists of the following scenario: The deployment spans over two larger areas connected by a narrow region, with a size of 6382   m 2 6382\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{2} and various obstacles. Our simulation assumes sporadic localization requests, e.g. individual agents wanting to navigate in the area. In addition to the locations of the 89 anchors, the environment is evaluated for 5, 20, and 100 tags. In order for the tags to calculate their positions, a minimum amount of 5 responses per tag is assumed. 
 The algorithms are compared, based on the following rationale: 
 FlexTDOA: 
 The FlexTDOA power consumption is independent of the distribution and number of tags. It depends on the FlexTDOA update frequency T F T_{F} and is evaluated for T F T_{F} = 60   ms 60\text{\,}\mathrm{ms} , 100   ms 100\text{\,}\mathrm{ms} , 1   s 1\text{\,}\mathrm{s} , 10   s 10\text{\,}\mathrm{s} and 100   s 100\text{\,}\mathrm{s} . 
 WakeLoc: 
 The anchor power consumption depends on the tag position. Therefore, 20 random tag placements were generated. For each tag placement and localization period (from 60   ms 60\text{\,}\mathrm{ms} to 10   s 10\text{\,}\mathrm{s} ), the power consumption was simulated by randomly selecting one of the tags as active while the others are passive - until each tag had performed 20 localizations ( passive and active combined). This leads to an average consumption for the tags and anchors for each placement and localization period. The WuC distance is considered to be up to 20   m 20\text{\,}\mathrm{m} . 
 AP-TWR: 
 The simulation of active AP-TWR is the same as the one of WakeLoc , except that no WuC is transmitted and the anchors’ transceivers’ are always on. 
 Significantly more different localization periods were simulated for WakeLoc and AP-TWR than for FlexTDOA . The reason for this is that FlexTDOA has a fixed localization period (which represents as well the latency), and in WakeLoc and AP-TWR the localizations happen asynchronously - modeled as random localization periods. The minimal localization period is 60   ms 60\text{\,}\mathrm{ms} , which is the shortest possible period possible when using the selected WuR , and is thus selected for FlexTDOA , AP-TWR and WakeLoc for comparability.

 V-A Localization Accuracy Characterization 
 In our experiments’ scope, a total of 9467 localizations were made across 70 different tag constellations. An overview of these results can be found in  Table   III . The localization results for active AP-TWR are the same as for active WakeLoc and are thus not explicitly listed. 
 In the x x - and y y -direction, both FlexTDOA and WakeLoc ( active and passive ) show an accuracy of less than 15   cm 15\text{\,}\mathrm{cm} , which is inline with other works  [ 20 , 14 , 21 ] . With an average error of − 3.0   cm -3.0\text{\,}\mathrm{cm} in x x -axis and − 1.0   cm -1.0\text{\,}\mathrm{cm} in y y -axis, the active tag in WakeLoc achieves the highest average 2D localization accuracy of 7.7   cm 7.7\text{\,}\mathrm{cm} . The standard deviation σ ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \sigma(\cdot) is 4.8   cm 4.8\text{\,}\mathrm{cm} . The passive WakeLoc tag performs slightly worse than the active tag with an average error of 9.1   cm 9.1\text{\,}\mathrm{cm} and a standard deviation of 6.7   cm 6.7\text{\,}\mathrm{cm} in 2D localization, as expected. The reason for this is that the error of the active tag is propagated into the result of the passive tag, as its estimation is used as ground-truth for the time calculations of the passive tag. With an average error of 12.9   cm 12.9\text{\,}\mathrm{cm} and a standard deviation of 6.8   cm 6.8\text{\,}\mathrm{cm} , the FlexTDOA performs worst in the 2D localization, with a standard deviation similar to that of the WakeLoc passive tag. 
 In z z -axis, the WakeLoc active tag has a large average error of 52.7   cm 52.7\text{\,}\mathrm{cm} with a standard deviation of 113.5   cm 113.5\text{\,}\mathrm{cm} . This results in a large error of 83.5   cm 83.5\text{\,}\mathrm{cm} in average with a standard deviation of 93.7   cm 93.7\text{\,}\mathrm{cm} for the 3D localization. This error could be improved by changing the z z -placement of the anchors   [ 22 ] . The error then propagates to the WakeLoc passive tag, resulting in a similar 3D localization error of 82.6   cm 82.6\text{\,}\mathrm{cm} in average. With the FlexTDOA scheme, the average error is 43.8   cm 43.8\text{\,}\mathrm{cm} with a standard deviation of 35   cm 35\text{\,}\mathrm{cm} . 
 However, it is notable that the localization of the WakeLoc passive tag is on par with the active tag: The reason for this is that the measurement noise of the active and passive tag are dependent random variables, since certain measurements ( Δ ​ T A i , t T ​ A i , … \Delta T_{A_{i}},\ t_{TA_{i}},\ \dots ) are the same. Therefore, measurement errors might even be reduced as more data is available. This is also the reason why WakeLoc passive can deliver better results than FlexTDOA , even though only an estimation is used for the position of the initiator instead of its exact location.

 The effect of the selective anchor wake-up can be clearly seen: depending on the number of tags and their localization frequency, the power consumption of each anchor using WakeLoc is up to 57.3 % 57.3\% lower than the power consumption of FlexTDOA : With 5 tags, T F = 100   ms T_{F}=$100\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}$ the consumption of an anchor in FlexTDOA is 1.68   mW 1.68\text{\,}\mathrm{mW} , whereas the expected consumption of an anchor in WakeLoc is only 717   µ ​ W 717\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro W} . This is a direct consequence of the large area deployment with many anchors and only few tags. With increasing number of tags, this advantage vanishes. For a worst case of 100 tags at T F = 1   s T_{F}=$1\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$ the anchors have active times similar to those of FlexTDOA , but have an additional cost of the WuR . 
 The anchors of AP-TWR are not competitive in any scenario: Although in all cases collisions occur with low localization periods below 332   ms 332\text{\,}\mathrm{ms} , the expected consumption per anchor increases logarithmically and converges to 120.9   mW 120.9\text{\,}\mathrm{mW} , as the idle receiver consumes more power than when packets are received and processed. 
 If we assume that the localization latency should be T F = 1   s T_{F}=$1\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$ , and the tags requesting updates every minute, FlexTDOA requires significantly more energy than WakeLoc : For 100 tags, the FlexTDOA anchors consume 178.5   µ ​ W 178.5\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro W} each, 11.5 × 11.5\times more than a WakeLoc anchor ( 15.53   µ ​ W 15.53\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro W} ). Therefore, WakeLoc anchors powered by a 690   mW 690\text{\,}\mathrm{mW} CR2023 battery could operate autonomously for up to 5.01 5.01 years, while a FlexTDOA anchor would be depleted after 161 161 days. 
 While as few tags as possible are beneficial for the anchors, for the tags the opposite can be observed: Fewer tags increase the number of active localizations per tag – which is associated with high costs for the wake-up: With 5 tags, T F = 1   s T_{F}=$1\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$ the power consumption is more than 12.3 × 12.3\times higher in WakeLoc than FlexTDOA and 10.5 × 10.5\times higher than AP-TWR . These values make a battery-powered tag with the assumed WuR impossible: Its runtime with the above battery would be limited to 7.7 7.7 hours. The more tags are available, the better the WuC get distributed between the individual tags, leading to a lower average consumption per tag. 
 To enable battery-powered tags, it is necessary to bring the tag consumption of WakeLoc closer to that of FlexTDOA (acting as a lower bound, see  Table   I ). One way to achieve this would be to use WuR that enable shorter wake-up patterns. With a decrease in wake-up duration from 55   ms 55\text{\,}\mathrm{ms} to 5   ms 5\text{\,}\mathrm{ms} , the advantage of FlexTDOA in the above example (5 tags, T F = 1   s T_{F}=$1\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$ ) would decrease from 12.3 × 12.3\times to 2.1 × 2.1\times . 
 VI Conclusion 
 In this paper we propose WakeLoc , an on-demand RTLS . It makes use of WuR and UWB -based measurements in order to offer both – ultra-low power consumption anchors as well as tags, while keeping the latency of the localization low.
In order to profile the novel WakeLoc scheme, it is compared to the state of the art FlexTDOA and AP-TWR , both in experimental evaluation as well as simulations for larger scale deployments. Real-world experiments showed localization accuracies comparable to state-of-the-art systems, achieving an average accuracy below 12.9   cm 12.9\text{\,}\mathrm{cm} in 2D localization. In terms of average anchor power requirement, WakeLoc clearly outperforms FlexTDOA by a reduction of 57.3 % 57.3\% for few tags, while approximating the power consumption of FlexTDOA for a large number of tags. AP-TWR is never competitive with WakeLoc . However, depending on the power consumption of the WuR , the tag’s power consumption is increased by WakeLoc . With the selective and on-demand-based system, anchors can be operated from a 690   mW 690\text{\,}\mathrm{mW} coin-cell battery for up to 5 years, whilest the RTLS performs on-demand localization with 5 tags and up to one localization per minute.
```
